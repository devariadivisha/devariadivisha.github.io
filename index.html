What ties it together

During my B.Tech I spent a week in the Netherlands studying how the Randstad plans for growth, water and mobility. I came home to work on roads designed around cars, while they were designing around people, bikes and water. That contrast is where my interest in how infrastructure decisions get made began. The courses, the IIT Kanpur degree, the bid tool and now the capstone all come back to it.

Research interests

Three questions I keep coming back to. Infrastructure procurement and financial risk: why cost and risk keep landing where they do on Indian projects, and what it would take to price carbon in at the tender stage. Data-driven decisions in capital-intensive settings: how to build tools that the people making real bid and investment calls will trust and use. Decarbonising the built environment through procurement: in a country that tenders new projects every day, the cheapest moment to cut embodied carbon is before the contract is signed.

Where I'm headed

Based in Abu Dhabi on a UAE residence visa, no sponsorship needed. Open to roles in infrastructure strategy, business development, data and climate-linked infrastructure, and to Master's and PhD programmes at the meeting point of infrastructure, data and decarbonisation.

Counting Carbon in Indian Transport Infrastructure Tenders

The problem. In Indian infrastructure, sustainability arrives after the decisions that fix a project's carbon footprint have already been made. Under lowest-bid award, contractors have no reason to measure carbon, and most have no means to either.

The proposal. A staged change led by the authority, which sets the baseline, not the contractor. It rewards before it requires, and gives contractors something to compete on besides lowest price.

Stage 1

Report

Contractors report embodied carbon from the existing bill of quantities, for a small bonus.

Stage 2

Reduce

A separate, larger bonus for real reduction, written into contract clauses.

Stage 3

Prequalify

Carbon performance becomes part of prequalification after 3 to 5 years.

Strategic Bid Intelligence Tool

Every tender a contractor looks at costs estimation time, and its bid security stays locked until the result is out. So choosing which tenders to chase is a business decision in itself. The tool answers one question before that money is spent: can we win this at a price we can live with?

It reads the tender PDF, predicts the range the winning bid is likely to land in against the authority's cost, and shows where RIPL's own estimate sits in that range. It then adds a weighted score for what a model can't see, such as competition, capacity and timing, and returns GO, CONDITIONAL GO or NO-GO with the reasoning written out.

What it can't do

An R² of 0.641 and a cross-validated score of 0.438 mean the model narrows the range; it doesn't call the exact number. Segments with little data fall back on historical averages. The tool has been used on 6 live tenders but isn't formally deployed, and the final call stays with the Technical Director.

Where it came from

It grew out of the Excel Go/No-Go framework I designed in 2022 and my IIT Kanpur capstone, which tested the idea on 120 live bids and showed me, honestly, where it fell short.

CapstoneMBA 944 · Grade A · supervised by Dr. Veena Bansal · solo

Predicting Competitive Bid Price for Infrastructure Projects

I modelled the awarded bid, as a percentage of the authority's cost, on 120 live bids across highway, railway and major structure projects (2020–2023). I compared linear regression, Ridge and KNN. Ridge did best, with a test R² of 0.677.

What the data showed: awarded bids averaged 17.4% below authority cost. EPC bids were the most aggressive of the contract modes, and projects in plain terrain drew sharper bids than those in hilly or water terrain.

The honest starting point. A cross-validation score of 0.44 told me the model didn't generalise yet. That result is where the Bid Intelligence Tool began: more data, recency weighting, and a decision layer on top.

PPP Construction Contracts: Financial Constraints and Mitigation

I studied six live BOT highway concessions through interviews with the people running them and a rebuild of each project's financials, then sorted their losses by cause. Land impediments accounted for 72% of the losses, far ahead of every other constraint.
